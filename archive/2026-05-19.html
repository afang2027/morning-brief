Art of the Day
A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884
Georges Seurat, French, 1859–1891
1884–86
Oil on canvas

Seurat built this monumental landscape through thousands of tiny dots of pure color, a technique he called chromoluminarism. The Seine riverbank scene — with its parasols, promenading figures, and still water — reads as a Sunday idyll, but the grid-like precision and frozen stillness give it an uncanny, almost architectural quality. It is the foundational work of Post-Impressionism, and every dot is a deliberate act.

Distant View of Niagara Falls
Thomas Cole, American, born England, 1801–1848
1830
Oil on panel

Cole painted Niagara from a distance that most artists avoided, foregrounding dense forest and open sky rather than the falls themselves. The water appears as a thin luminous band at the horizon, dwarfed by the sublime American landscape surrounding it. This framing — where human drama recedes before the scale of the continent — became the defining gesture of the Hudson River School.

Mountain Brook
Albert Bierstadt, American, born Germany, 1830–1902
1863
Oil on canvas

Bierstadt painted this intimate mountain stream as a counterpoint to his enormous panoramic canvases of the American West. The close framing and dappled light through the trees give it a contemplative quality quite different from his famous Yosemite vistas. The brook itself — clear, cold, and unhurried — carries the same luminous attention he gave to entire mountain ranges.

Pastoral Landscape with Ruins
Adriaen van de Velde, Dutch, 1636–1672
1664
Oil on panel

Van de Velde animates this classical ruin landscape with staffage figures — the shepherds, cattle, and travelers that Dutch Golden Age painters used to give scale and life to otherwise still scenes. The crumbling architecture recedes into golden afternoon light, a reminder that even monuments are pastoral eventually. His figures are painted with the same careful attention as the sky.
