Art of the Day
Nighthawks
Edward Hopper (American, 1882–1967)
1942
Oil on canvas

Hopper placed three customers and a server inside an all-night diner, their figures isolated by the geometry around them — no entrance, no debris on the streets, nothing extraneous to the composition. The warm glow of electric light set against the dark exterior creates a tension between warmth and isolation, visibility and disconnection. This Modernist arrangement, spare in its subjects of architecture, people, and view through glass, renders an ordinary urban scene both serene and quietly enigmatic.

Blue and Green Music
Georgia O’Keeffe (American, 1887–1986)
1919–21
Oil on canvas

Painted to explore “the idea that music could be translated into something for the eye,” O’Keeffe used color and abstracted form to simultaneously suggest the natural world and the experience of sound. Working within Modernism’s rejection of strict representation, she drew on Vasily Kandinsky’s argument that visual art could achieve pure expression free of literary reference. Without named subjects, blues and greens shift between landscape and abstraction, occupying the threshold between what is seen and what is heard.

Thanksgiving
Doris Lee (American, 1904–1983)
c. 1935
Oil on canvas

Painted in a deliberately cartoon-like Modernist manner, this bustling oil on canvas depicts women preparing a Thanksgiving feast and became a national flashpoint when it won the Art Institute’s prestigious Logan Purchase Prize in 1935. The prize donor condemned the broad, exaggerated style as “too modern,” founding the conservative “Sanity in Art” movement in protest — a reaction that backfired and brought Illinois-born Doris Lee national fame. Executed during the Great Depression, the subject matter of rural custom, family, and holiday celebration carried particular resonance for struggling Americans.

Picture of Dorian Gray
Ivan Albright (American, 1897–1983)
1943–44
Oil on canvas

Chicago artist Ivan Albright painted this grisly portrait for the 1945 film adaptation of Oscar Wilde’s novel, where it appeared in vivid Technicolor against the otherwise black-and-white film — a deliberately shocking contrast. Working across both Surrealism and Modernism, Albright rendered the subjects of portraiture and people through a lens of macabre fascination, creating an image designed to simultaneously attract and repulse. When the portrait was exhibited at the Art Institute, the Chicago Tribune reported the museum was “having a heck of a time handling the crowds flocking to see his painting.”
